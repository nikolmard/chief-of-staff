What I believe

It doesn't have to be this way.

I've worked in companies of fewer than 20 people and more than 10,000, in Norway, Ireland and Czechia, with remote teams across Asia and the Americas. Tech, manufacturing, energy, non-profit and startups. They all had one thing in common: information didn't reach the right people at the right time in the right format. That broke trust. Broken trust led to bottlenecks, delays, costly mistakes and burnout.

I was told that's just the way companies are. That managing impressions is smart, that leaders need to be ruthless, that "divide and conquer" is a valid strategy.

We don't need to choose between being human and being professional. We don't need to deliver results at the cost of our wellbeing. And we don't need to be pitted against each other to succeed.

People-first Chief of Staff

Stop wishing you could clone yourself.

It's all in your head. My job is to get it out: structured, shared, and running without you having to explain it every time.

Your company is growing fast. What worked as unspoken rules in a small team now needs to become process. New people join, meetings multiply, and following up becomes impossible. Things fall through the cracks, and there's a nagging feeling that you've forgotten something.

The new leadership hire hasn't had enough of your time. The board deck is ready, but one department head's input is half-cooked because you only exchanged a couple of messages. Your people matter to you, but you rarely have time for more than a quick chat. So you put on a brave face and add "people development" to the list for when things calm down. They never do.

Doing everything yourself creates a bottleneck. Delegating everything means you lose the overview. More headcount doesn't fix it, and AI can automate the back end but not people working with people. What's missing is the connecting tissue across strategy, operations and people.

"I wish there was someone who could pick up all the pieces, someone who can be in the rooms I can't be in, someone with whom I don't have to pretend I'm on top of everything." The thought that usually comes right before a founder calls me.
If I started tomorrow

Most people start with the business. I start with people, because people are the business.

  1. Capture what's in your head

    I sit with you closely enough to pull out the thinking that's currently only yours: what needs to happen, who needs to be involved, how and when.

How not to waste each other's time

What I ask for from day one

A real mandate, not "special projects"

Named decision rights, agreed before I start. A vague mandate is the most common reason this kind of role fails.

Visibility to your whole leadership team

The people I coordinate need to know why I have a mandate and what it covers, or every conversation starts from scratch.

A measurable outcome at 90 days

Specific enough that we'd both recognise whether it's working, and can talk honestly and early if it isn't.

How we start

A 3-month paid pilot, before either of us commits to more

Three outcomes by week 12

What the pilot delivers

Executive operating rhythm. A weekly leadership meeting structure, a one-page priorities view and a decision log, running without me by week 12.

One cross-functional initiative. I own coordination of one initiative, from plan to first results.

Founder leverage. A short list of recurring tasks and decisions moved off your plate, agreed in week 2.

Fixed hours, clear boundaries

How we work together

A fixed block of hours each week on set days, a 30-minute weekly check-in with you, one leadership meeting, and a short written update every Friday on progress, blockers and decisions needed.

We review at week 6 and week 12. Then you choose: an ongoing retainer, an extension, or a clean handover of every document and process.

Success means you spend less time on coordination, priorities are visible to the whole leadership team, and work moves without constant chasing.

Beneath the Role

For leaders who want teams built on real connection, and know that starts with knowing yourself.

Most leadership development hands people three steps to this or ten points to that. I place self-awareness at the foundation: who we are, what shaped us, and how we show up. Without that foundation, even the best frameworks lack depth. You can't build a house without one.

As Gabor Maté describes, from childhood most of us learn to suppress our authentic selves to stay connected to the people we depend on. We carry that adaptation into our careers and into the rooms where we lead. The child who kept the peace at the dinner table becomes the manager who can't handle conflict in a meeting.

Which parts of the way I show up are genuinely mine, and which am I performing because I learned they were required?

When leaders work from that awareness, they stop managing impressions and start seeing each other as whole human beings. In an age where AI handles more of the work, that capacity to see and be seen is what sets us apart.

About Nikol

I get people aligned quickly because I look at them as whole human beings, not just the mask of their role.

I ask, and I really listen, with genuine interest in people's experience at work and outside it. I build trust by making room for emotions in places where feelings are considered unprofessional. I factor human behaviour, motivations and needs into every strategy, because it's people who deliver it. The biggest gap is usually between work as imagined and work as done.

Working this way, against the supposed norms, has given me consistent results: less friction and better engagement, so work gets done faster. Better decisions, because people feel safe to speak their mind. And a healthier atmosphere where people can be themselves.
